# Drill — foundational script orthography

**Goal:** internalize the Arabic script itself, below the level of root/pattern or iʿrāb, before those skills are
asked to carry any weight. Every later drill assumes the reader can already tell confusable letters apart,
segment a word by its connectivity classes, read the short-vowel and vocalization-state marks, hold sukūn and
shadda apart, read wāw/yāʾ correctly as consonant or long-vowel carrier, realize nunation's written form, and
apply the definite article's assimilation rule. This drill makes that foundation explicit and practiceable on its
own, rather than leaving it as an unstated assumption behind every other lesson.

**Rule of the drill:** an answer here is objective and closed — a confusable pair, a connectivity class, a mark's
position, a mark's meaning, or a class membership either is or is not what the item states. Say what the shapes
or marks actually are, not what they resemble; a family resemblance, a shared silhouette, or a familiar sound is
never itself the evidence.

---

## 1. Grapheme inventory and confusables

Two letters can look alike for two different reasons: they may share one skeleton and differ only by their dots
(a within-family contrast), or they may belong to two different families that simply happen to have a similar
overall silhouette (a cross-family contrast, settled by stroke shape or articulation, not by dots at all).

### 1. Two hook-shaped letters, two different families
**Letters:** د vs ر
**Answer:** different families, distinguished by the shape and depth of the stroke below the line, not by dots —
neither letter carries one.

### 2. A plain/emphatic pair kept apart
**Letters:** س vs ص
**Answer:** two separate phonemes; the emphatic member's pharyngealization changes the surrounding vowel quality,
so the pair is learned and rehearsed together, never merged into one sound.

### 3. One skeleton, one dot signature
**Letters:** ب vs ت (family: ب ت ث ن ي)
**Answer:** identical skeleton; dot count and side carry the entire contrast, and must be read before the letter
is named.

### 4. Two articulations, two letters
**Letters:** هـ vs حـ
**Answer:** a light glottal sound and a pharyngeal fricative produced further back; separate letters, never one
letter pronounced two ways.

## 2. Orthographic connectivity classes

Every letter is fixed, once and for all, as connecting (attaches to the following letter) or non-connecting
(never does). That fixed fact produces the visual runs and gaps a word is written in, and it is what a medial
letterform actually looks like once the skeleton is shared across a family.

### 5. The non-connecting set blocks attachment
**Word:** دَم
**Answer:** د is in the closed non-connecting set and never attaches forward, whatever letter follows it; the
rule is fixed by the letter, not decided by context.

### 6. The baseline is part of the letterform
**Item:** a word whose letters drift off one level line
**Answer:** attachment position is defined against a shared baseline; losing that level line destroys the cue
that separates an attachment stroke from the start of a new letter.

### 7. A gap is not automatically a word boundary
**Word:** وَلَد
**Answer:** the letter before a gap must be checked against the non-connecting set before the gap is read as the
end of a word; و is a non-connector, so the gap here is expected inside one word.

### 8. Medial teeth, told apart only by dots
**Letters:** سـ vs شـ (medial position)
**Answer:** the medial tooth shapes are genuinely identical; only the three dots above شـ distinguish it from سـ.

## 3. Short-vowel diacritics and vocalization state

The three short-vowel marks are read by position, not shape, and a written position carries a vowel only when a
mark actually licenses one there. Most ordinary text is unmarked; that is the normal case, not a defect.

### 9. Position, not shape, decides a-mark vs i-mark
**Marks:** fatḥa vs kasra
**Answer:** the same stroke shape; fatḥa sits above the letter (short a), kasra sits below it (short i) — the
placement is read before the vowel is assigned.

### 10. Unmarked text is the ordinary case
**Item:** an ordinary text with no vowel marks at all
**Answer:** unmarked writing is normal, not incomplete; the reader supplies the short vowels from pattern and
context rather than stalling in front of the missing marks.

### 11. No mark, no inserted vowel
**Item:** two consonants written back to back with no vowel mark between them
**Answer:** a vowel is licensed only by an actual written mark; inserting one for convenience changes the
syllable count and risks a different word.

### 12. The u-mark is short; length needs a carrier
**Mark:** ḍamma
**Answer:** ḍamma alone marks a short u; the long ū needs an added, unmarked wāw carrier letter immediately
after it — the mark by itself never signals length.

## 4. Sukūn and shadda

Sukūn and shadda are opposite instructions, both written as small superscript marks over a consonant: sukūn
forbids a following vowel, shadda doubles the consonant and is read together with whatever vowel is stacked on
it.

### 13. Sukūn is a prohibition, not a blank
**Mark:** sukūn (the small circle)
**Answer:** a positive instruction that forbids a following vowel and closes the syllable; pronouncing a vowel
after it directly contradicts the mark.

### 14. Sukūn's circle vs shadda's head-shaped stroke
**Marks:** sukūn vs shadda
**Answer:** sukūn (closed circle) forbids a vowel on a single consonant; shadda (head-shaped stroke) doubles the
consonant and keeps a following vowel — opposite instructions, not variant spellings of one idea.

### 15. Gemination is held length, not emphasis
**Mark:** shadda
**Answer:** the consonant is held for two consonant slots; that length is contrastive, so pronouncing it once
substitutes a different word, not a lighter version of the same one.

### 16. Read the vowel stacked on the shadda
**Item:** a shadda-marked consonant with a vowel mark stacked on it
**Answer:** the stacked vowel determines the syllable that follows the doubled consonant; realizing the doubling
alone and dropping the vowel loses half the instruction.

## 5. Long-vowel carrier role

Wāw and yāʾ are each ambiguous between a full consonant and a long-vowel carrier; the mark configuration, not
habit, decides which role applies in a given word. Vowel length itself is contrastive.

### 17. A dotless word-final yāʾ-shape spells long ā
**Grapheme:** ى
**Answer:** a positional variant spelling of alif maqṣūra (long ā), not a yāʾ, even though it shares yāʾ's
skeleton by convention; treat it as the a-spelling absent specific contrary evidence.

### 18. Consonant or carrier — read it from the marks
**Letters:** و and ي
**Answer:** an own vowel mark signals the consonant role; being unmarked right after the matching short vowel
signals the long-vowel-carrier role — decided case by case from the marks, never assumed fixed for the letter.

### 19. Length is contrastive, not a speaking style
**Item:** shortening a long vowel in speech
**Answer:** a long vowel and its short counterpart are distinct phonemes; shortening one in speech risks
substituting a different word, not merely speaking faster.

## 6. Nunation's written realization

Nunation is a doubled short-vowel mark at a word's final edge, never realized the same way as gemination on a
stem consonant. Its a-form additionally needs a silent supporting alif on an ordinary consonant-final word,
decided from the word's own final grapheme, not by ear.

### 20. Locate the mark: word edge or stem consonant?
**Marks:** nunation vs gemination
**Answer:** nunation sits at the word's final edge and adds a nasal ending; gemination sits over a stem consonant
and doubles it — position, not appearance, tells them apart.

### 21. The supporting alif is silent
**Item:** the a-form of nunation with its supporting alif
**Answer:** the alif is an orthographic residue with no sound of its own; the ending is read as vowel plus nasal,
and nothing more.

### 22. The support rule is read from the final grapheme
**Item:** deciding whether a word needs the supporting alif
**Answer:** checked against the word's own final grapheme and the short list of exempt endings, never decided by
how the ending sounds; outside the exemptions, the support is required.

## 7. Definite-article assimilation

The article's lām assimilates only before the closed set of sun letters, merging into the following consonant as
gemination rather than being deleted; before every moon letter it stays audible. Class membership is fixed and
memorized, never derived from spelling or extended by analogy.

### 23. No gemination on a moon letter, ever
**Item:** the article before a moon letter
**Answer:** gemination is licensed only for the closed sun-letter set; before a moon letter the lām stays audible
and is pronounced as an ordinary consonant, with no extension by analogy.

### 24. Assimilation merges, it does not delete
**Item:** the article before a sun letter
**Answer:** the lām's material is preserved as the lengthening of the following consonant; dropping the lām
without geminating that consonant treats a merger as a deletion.

### 25. Sun and moon are closed, memorized sets
**Item:** deciding a letter's sun/moon membership
**Answer:** two fixed lists; nothing in a word's spelling reveals membership on the spot, so the lists are
memorized and checked, anchored on the shared articulatory generalization for the sun letters.

### 26. The lām is orthographic-only before a sun letter
**Item:** pronouncing the article's lām before a sun letter
**Answer:** the following letter's class decides pronunciation, not the fact that a lām is written; before a sun
letter the written lām is silent, its material realized as gemination instead.

the feature is not a pass.
